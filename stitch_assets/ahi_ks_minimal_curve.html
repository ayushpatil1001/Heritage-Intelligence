On 9th December 1948, during the Constituent Assembly Debates on Draft Article 25 (culminating in Article 32 of the final Constitution), Dr. B.R. Ambedkar firmly rebuffed propositions to dilute prerogative constitutional writs. He underscored that remedies are not mere procedure, but the substantive life-force of democratic liberties.

"If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important—an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity—I could not refer to any other article except this one. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it..."

Babasaheb argued that while Fundamental Rights articulate democratic guarantees, without the Writs of Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, and Certiorari directly enforceable in the Supreme Court, constitutional declarations would remain ephemeral promises.

Constituent Assembly of India Debates

Volume VII • Thursday, 9th December 1948 • Council House, New Delhi

PAGES 952–954
Record #491

The Honourable Dr. B. R. Ambedkar: Mr. Vice-President, Sir, of the amendments that have been moved to this article, I can accept only No. 786 by Mr. Kamath. Regarding the other amendments, I feel compelled to state my respectful disagreement.

The general debate on this article has turned largely on the question of whether this article is redundant in view of the remedies already provided in the judicial system or whether it constitutes an unnecessary limitation upon the rights of Parliament.

Now, Sir, I am very glad that the majority of those who have spoken on this article have realized the great significance and importance of it. It is difficult to conceive of any constitution which could omit a provision of this character.

A right without a remedy is a legal fiction. If rights are conferred by the Constitution, the Constitution must itself simultaneously provide the machinery for their immediate enforcement.
